Midair collision · January 29, 2025

An airliner and an Army helicopter collide over the Potomac

At about 8:48 p.m. Eastern time, PSA Airlines flight 5342, a CRJ700 regional jet arriving from Wichita, Kansas, and a US Army Sikorsky UH-60L helicopter flying as PAT25 collided about half a mile southeast of Ronald Reagan Washington National Airport (DCA). Both aircraft fell into the Potomac River.

All 64 people aboard the airplane and all 3 crew members aboard the helicopter were killed. Both aircraft were destroyed.

This page sets out the facts in the National Transportation Safety Board’s preliminary report. The report does not state a cause, and the NTSB’s investigation is ongoing. Times are Eastern Standard Time.

According to FAA aeronautical information specialists, helicopter routes have no defined lateral boundaries. Equipment

Collision warnings: TCAS and ADS-B

TCAS (traffic alert and collision avoidance system) is a family of airborne devices that work independently of ground-based air traffic control. The FAA requires TCAS II on commercial aircraft, including regional airliners with more than 30 seats or a maximum takeoff weight over 33,000 lbs.

TCAS II gives two kinds of alert, shown on the instrument panel and spoken aloud when its computer predicts a conflict. A traffic advisory is a warning; on the CRJ it is announced as “TRAFFIC - TRAFFIC.” A resolution advisory recommends an escape maneuver in the vertical dimension, to increase or keep the vertical separation between aircraft, with calls such as “CLIMB, CLIMB,” “DESCEND, DESCEND” or “LEVEL OFF, LEVEL OFF.”

ADS-B, according to the FAA, uses GPS signals to work out an aircraft’s precise position and broadcasts it, with other data, to other aircraft and ATC facilities. The airplane had ADS-B out; the helicopter’s transponder was capable of it.

What TCAS does on the way down

Below 900 ft above ground on descent
Resolution advisories are inhibited. A traffic advisory comes when the other aircraft is about 20 seconds from closest approach, or 0.3 nm away, whichever happens first.
Below 400 ft above ground on arrival
The spoken part of the traffic advisory is inhibited.

In this accident: at 8:47:40, with the aircraft about 0.95 nm apart, flight 5342’s TCAS announced “Traffic, Traffic.”

Evidence

Flight recorders

Flight 5342

A separate flight data recorder (FDR) and cockpit voice recorder (CVR), both L3-Harris FA2100 solid-state units. An FBI dive team recovered them; data from both downloaded successfully.

  • CVR: about 2 hours 4 minutes of good to excellent audio, starting mid-flight because it holds 2 hours.
  • FDR: the whole flight, about 400 parameters.

PAT25

One combined voice and data recorder: a Penny & Giles Multi-Purpose Flight Recorder inside a Goodrich Integrated Vehicle Health Management Unit. The Metropolitan Police Department of the District of Columbia recovered it.

  • Voice: about 2 hours of excellent audio.
  • Data: the whole flight, about 70 parameters. By design it records no date, time or GPS position.
  • A parameter documented as “barometric altitude” was actually pressure altitude. Its values were inconsistent with other data and it was declared invalid. Further work will determine whether the problem was limited to the recorder or may have affected other helicopter systems that use pressure altitude.

Recovery and examination

Wreckage

Both aircraft came down in the Potomac in water 1 to 8 ft deep, depending on the tide. The Army Corps of Engineers and the US Navy Supervisor of Salvage and Diving Operations (SUPSALV) surveyed the site with sonar and divers wearing helmet cameras. SUPSALV recovered the aircraft under NTSB supervision, and the wreckage went to a secured hangar at DCA.

  Airplane examination

Extensive water impact damage to most of the structure. The fuselage was recovered in 13 major sections covering its full length, from the radome in the nose to the tail cone.

Lower right aft fuselage fairing: a puncture about 10 by 8 inches, and a slash about 29 inches long and 2 inches wide through the fairing, internal structure and lavatory access door. An approximately 2-ft section of one of the helicopter’s tail rotor blades was embedded in the slash.

Tail: the vertical stabilizer and rudder stayed attached; the horizontal stabilizer and elevators separated from its top.

Right wing: largely intact and attached to part of the center fuselage, slats and flaps fully extended, landing gear attached and extended.

Left wing: the inboard section was significantly fragmented; the outboard section from wing station 178 to the tip separated and was found in the debris field. The inboard end of the left center slat had a linear slash about 52 inches long, with the skin behind it gouged and abraded aftward; the forward spar was cut along the same diagonal line and the structure inside was deformed aft and inboard, with some black paint transfer. Two other inboard left slat sections showed denting and black paint transfer. The left main gear’s outboard tire had a diagonal slash through all its layers.

Fibrous material was found in several sections, including the left wing and vertical stabilizer; samples went to the NTSB Materials Laboratory.

Engines: neither nacelle showed evidence of fire or uncontainment. The left engine separated from its pylon, core mostly intact, with all fan blades bent aft and their leading edges dented, gouged and missing material. The right engine was mostly intact on its pylon; its fan blades were full length and bent opposite the direction of rotation, except one fractured about midspan. Blades 10, 12, 14, 16, 19 and 22 had localized deformation near their tips, and part of the fan case rub strip was rubbed away from the 8 to 11 o’clock positions.

Kept for further examination: the TCAS transmitter receiver, communication management unit, transponder, air data computers and exterior light bulbs.

  Helicopter examination

The helicopter came to rest inverted, its tail structure separated from the fuselage around the tail transition section. The main fuselage had fractured into multiple pieces, held together during recovery mainly by cabling, hoses and remnant structure. Both internal crash-resistant fuel tanks were in place; the right one was partially detached.

Tail: the tail boom was intact with wrinkled skin; the vertical fin was mostly intact except its upper end, fractured and bent to the right; the stabilator showed forward impact deformation. The tail rotor hub plates stayed on the gearbox output shaft. One of four tail rotor blades was mostly intact; remnants of the other three remained in the hub.

Main rotor: the main gearbox stayed attached to the roof beams, which had separated from the fuselage. The hub stayed on the rotor shaft. All four main rotor blades were fractured near the hub.

Cockpit: heavily fragmented. The left seat barometric altimeter’s setting window read between 29.88 and 29.89 inches of mercury; the right seat altimeter was set to 29.87. Both barometric altimeters, both radar altimeters and their antennas, and the electronic standby instrument system were kept for examination.

Pitot-static system: highly fragmented. Fittings checked were tight, except one pitot outlet line fitting found near the cockpit nose wreckage; that line and the two recovered probes were kept for examination.

Engines: neither showed evidence of external fire or uncontainment. The aft portion of the left engine was recovered intact, and dive teams recovered further pieces, including the accessory gearbox and parts of the compressor; the helicopter drive shaft was fractured in front of the engine’s output drive assembly. The right engine appeared intact, and turning its output shaft by hand turned the power turbine smoothly.

Also kept: the transponder and its remote control unit (maintenance records and the wreckage confirm the helicopter had a transponder), and two iPad minis found apart from the main wreckage.

Before January 29

Earlier close calls at DCA

DCA, operated by the Metropolitan Washington Airports Authority, is the 24th busiest airport in the United States. Because of noise regulations, flight patterns follow the Potomac or Anacostia rivers.

Voluntary reports and FAA data, 2011 to 2024

The vast majority of reported encounters between helicopters and commercial aircraft near DCA happened on approach to landing. Initial analysis found at least one TCAS resolution advisory a month triggered by a nearby helicopter. In over half of those, the helicopter may have been above the route altitude restriction. Two-thirds of the events happened at night.

Runway 15 handled about 5% of departures. Flight 5342 had been switched to runway 33.

The runways

DCA has three operational runways. Runway 1/19, the most used, is 7,169 ft long and 150 ft wide. Runway 15/33, the second most used, is 5,204 ft long and 150 ft wide. Runway 33 has a four-light precision approach path indicator (PAPI) giving a 3° visual approach path to the touchdown zone; it was working at the time of the accident.

Since the accident

Actions since

  1. January 31, 2025
    The FAA issued a Notice to Airmen (NOTAM) prohibiting helicopters from operating over the Potomac near DCA from the surface up to and including 17,999 ft msl. Exceptions: lifesaving medical, active law enforcement, air defense, and presidential transport missions. When such a mission is flown, civilian aircraft are not allowed in the area.
  2. February 19, 2025
    The most recent version, NOTAM 5/4379, updated the dimensions of the temporary flight restriction. It is in effect through March 31, 2025, at 23:59 UTC.
  3. March 11, 2025
    The NTSB issued two urgent safety recommendations to the FAA about the helicopter routes near DCA.
