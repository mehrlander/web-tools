Voters to decide if WA Cares tax dollars can be invested in stock market

A proposed constitutional amendment would let assets be managed like the state’s pension funds. A similar attempt failed in 2020.

Jerry Cornfield, Washington State Standard, October 31, 2025

What voters are deciding

The proposal would add a few lines to the state constitution that would change how billions of taxpayer dollars are managed. It seeks to let the state make stock market investments with payroll taxes collected for its long-term care program, known as WA Cares.

  • 1The Washington State Investment Board would be legally able to handle the program’s assets in a similar manner as it does pension and retirement funds.
  • 2Any earnings would have to be used solely for the program’s beneficiaries.
  • 3It will become law if more than 50% of voters approve it on Tuesday.

What would change

Today

Bonds and other fixed income only

The state constitution generally bars investing public money in the stock of private companies.

That means state and local governments are limited to fixed-income securities like government bonds, treasury bills and certificates of deposit, which are less risky but typically deliver lower rates of return.

The WA Cares account is now subject to this limitation.

If approved

Managed like the pension funds

The long-term service account would be added to the list of funds exempt from that prohibition, alongside the state’s pension and retirement funds.

The State Investment Board could then decide if it wants to invest in private equity funds and buy stock of private companies, or not.

Money made from investments must go into the WA Cares Fund.

The two sides

Both spoke at a recent debate at the University of Washington.

For

Heather Weiner

Approve 8201 campaign

She said the measure will allow for the tax dollars to be invested in a “safe and risk averse way” while producing greater returns for the program. In the long run, she said, the higher returns of the pension funds would translate to a lot more dollars for the program.

This doesn’t mean day trading, taking big risks on a stock market. This means smart long-term [investing] so that in 50 years when people in this audience today will need long term care, that fund will be there for you.

She said this year’s proposal, unlike the one in 2020, mandates that money made from investments must go into the WA Cares Fund. The intent is to prevent lawmakers from tapping those dollars for other budget needs.

Against

State Sen. Bob Hasegawa

D-Tukwila

Our fiduciary responsibility is to the people. No one is going to know when the economy or stock market crashes. So, this vote is about whether we keep prudent fiscal management policies of our tax dollars and retain those constitutional protections.

Hasegawa said there is no funded opposition campaign, just like the last ballot fight. He and three Republican state lawmakers penned the voters’ pamphlet statement opposing the measure.

WA Cares and this measure, in order

  1. 2019

    A state law creates WA Cares, funded with a 0.58% tax on the paychecks of most workers in Washington.

  2. 2020

    Voters defeat a similar measure. It passed in only three Washington counties: King, Jefferson and Whatcom.

  3. Mid-2023

    Collections of the payroll tax begin.

  4. 2025

    The Legislature votes to put the constitutional amendment on the ballot, 86–9 in the House and 42–7 in the Senate. As of summer, more than $2.5 billion is banked in the Long Term Services and Supports Trust Account.

  5. Tuesday

    Voters decide on Senate Joint Resolution 8201. It becomes law with more than 50% approval.

  6. January

    A pilot program starts in a handful of counties.

  7. July 2026

    Those who qualify can begin accessing the benefit, a lifetime amount of $36,500 that is set to rise in future years to account for inflation.

Underwriting the campaign. The union represents 55,000 long-term care workers in Washington, Montana and Alaska, and had reported raising and spending $2.5 million as of Friday, five times more than it spent in a losing effort in 2020.

Opposing the measure
